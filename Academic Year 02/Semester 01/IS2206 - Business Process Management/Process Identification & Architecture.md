# What is Process Identification?

**Process Identification** is a systematic set of activities aimed at defining an organization's business processes and establishing clear criteria to prioritize them.

- **Primary Output:** The result of this phase is a **Process Architecture**, which serves as a framework for specifying the scope of future modeling and redesign projects.
- **Core Objective:** It answers critical questions such as: _What processes are we executing? Which ones meet our goals? Which ones need the most improvement?_.
# The Two Key Phases of Identification

The identification process is divided into two major phases:
## A. Designation Phase

The goal here is to gain an understanding of all processes and their interrelationships.

- **Process Enumeration:** This involves listing all existing processes. It requires a trade-off: ensure the scope is **manageable** (not too broad) but also large enough to have a **significant impact** when improved.
- **Broad vs. Narrow Processes:**
    - **Broad processes** are those targeted for a complete overhaul of operations.
    - **Narrow processes** are not for major overhauls but require active monitoring and continuous fine-tuning.
- **Process Boundaries:** Since processes are interdependent and change over time, identification must be an **exploratory and iterative** activity.
## B. Evaluation Phase

Once processes are listed, they must be prioritized using three main criteria:

1. **Importance:** Which processes have the greatest impact on the company’s strategic goals?.
2. **Dysfunction:** Which processes are currently in the most "trouble" or have the poorest health?.
3. **Feasibility:** How susceptible is the process to a BPM initiative? (Is it easy or hard to change?).
# Designing a Process Architecture

A **Process Architecture** is a conceptual model that makes the relationships between processes explicit. These relationships are generally:

- **Sequential:** One process provides an output that another process uses as an input (Producer-Consumer relationship).
- **Hierarchical:** Core business processes are decomposed into smaller sub-processes down to the task level.
## The 3-Level Architecture (The Pyramid)

Organizations typically organize their architecture into three levels of detail:

- **Level 1: Process Landscape:** A very abstract view showing main processes and their relationships.
- **Level 2: Abstract Process Models:** Shows processes at a finer degree of granularity (often called a **Process Map**).
- **Level 3: Detailed Process Models:** The most granular level, showing control flows, data inputs/outputs, and specific participants (often using **BPMN**).
# The Dijkman Approach

To build this architecture, your lectures highlight the **Dijkman Approach**, which follows four steps:

1. **Identify Case Types:** Determine what the organization handles (Products, Services, Channels, or Customer types).
2. **Identify Functions:** Use interviews and reference models (like ITIL or APQC) to list what the organization does.
3. **Construct Case/Function Matrices:** Create a matrix where case types are columns and functions are rows.
4. **Identify Processes:** Determine which combinations of functions and cases form a single business process using **eight guidelines** (e.g., splitting processes by different flow objects, transactional states, or geographic locations).
# Benefits of a Clear Architecture

Having a well-defined process architecture allows an organization to:

- Align daily activities with **business strategies**.
- Identify opportunities for **automation**.
- Predict the impact of **system changes**.
- Spot **bottlenecks** and delays clearly.
