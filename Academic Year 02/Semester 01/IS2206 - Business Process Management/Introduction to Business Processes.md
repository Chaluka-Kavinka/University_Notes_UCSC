# Defining a Business Process

A **business process** is a collection of related events, activities, and decisions that involve a number of actors and resources. These elements collectively lead to an **outcome** that provides **value** to an organization or its customers.

- **Key Characteristics:** It consists of a set of coordinated activities performed within an organizational and technical environment to realize a specific business goal.
- **Organizational Context:** While a process is typically enacted by a single organization, it may interact with processes performed by other organizations.
- **Common Examples:**
    - **University context:** Admission, curriculum development, evaluations, teaching, and payroll.
    - **General business context:** Order-to-cash, quote-to-order, procure-to-pay, application-to-approval, and issue-to-resolution.
# Elements of a Business Process

To understand or model a process, you must identify its core components:

- **Events:** These happen automatically and have no duration. They often trigger the execution of a series of activities (e.g., the arrival of equipment at a site).
- **Activities:** Work initiated by an event. An activity can be complex, involving many steps (e.g., an engineer inspecting equipment).
- **Tasks:** A task is a simple activity viewed as a single unit of work (e.g., checking that the received equipment matches the order).
- **Decision Points:** Points in time when a choice is made that affects how the process is executed (e.g., deciding whether to return equipment after inspection).
- **Actors:** The people, organizations, or software systems acting on their behalf involved in the process.
- **Objects:** These can be **physical** (equipment, products, paper documents) or **immaterial** (electronic records and documents).
- **Outcomes:** The result of process execution.
    - **Positive Outcome:** Delivers value to the actors.
    - **Negative Outcome:** Fails to deliver the intended value.
- **Sub-processes:** Self-contained, composite activities that can be broken down into smaller units of work.
# Outcome vs. Value

Understanding the distinction between these two is critical for process improvement:

- **Outcome:** The measurable results and what your product/service specifically does for the customer.
- **Value:** What you promise to the customer to solve their specific problems or "pain points".
- **The Relationship:** They are symbiotic; while an outcome provides a result, value is only unlocked when the right outcome is prescribed at the right time based on the customer’s specific goals.
# What is Business Process Management (BPM)?

BPM is described as both an **art and a science**. It involves overseeing how work is performed in an organization to ensure consistent outcomes and to capitalize on improvement opportunities.

- **Typical Objectives:** Reducing execution times, lowering costs, and minimizing error rates.
- **Drivers of BPM:** Efficiency (improving performance), Compliance (standardizing processes), Quality (reducing exceptions), Agility (responsiveness), and Integration (connecting operations with IT).
- **Related Disciplines:** BPM shares goals with Total Quality Management (TQM), Operations Management, Lean, and Six Sigma.
# Categories of Business Processes

Processes are generally categorized into three main types:

1. **Core Processes:** Essential operations that directly deliver value to the organization and its customers, such as sales, production, manufacturing, and marketing.
2. **Support Processes:** "Back-office" functions that support core processes. They provide value to internal customers and do not generate income directly (e.g., HR, IT support, accounting).
3. **Management Processes:** Used to plan, organize, and control resources to ensure the organization meets financial and operational goals (e.g., strategic planning, budgeting, compliance).
## Organizational Scope:

- **Functional:** Processes that happen within a single department (e.g., "Hiring & Training" within Human Resources).
- **Cross-Functional:** Processes where multiple departments work together toward a common goal (e.g., processing a customer order involves sales, finance, and delivery).
